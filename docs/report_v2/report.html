Three solvers, one heat sink: which solver for which 3D thermal problem

Candidate ID: RA2311042010060 · Name: Aakash G S · Repository: github.com/ftaakash/UniForge, branch v2 · Reply to the Assignment Extension Notice · November 2026

1. Summary

Question. Apply three different types of solver to the same steady thermal analysis, on an object complex enough that they differ, and say which is most efficient and when.

Object. HS-7, a seven-fin aluminium heat sink on a silicon die with a 0.1 mm thermal interface layer (TIM), cooled by convection. It is meshed with trilinear hexahedra from 56 015 to 3 759 720 unknowns (DOF).

Solvers, all written for this study on one shared sparse-matrix layer: A: sparse direct Cholesky (two orderings), B: preconditioned conjugate gradient (Jacobi and incomplete-Cholesky preconditioners) and C: smoothed-aggregation algebraic multigrid (stand-alone and as a CG preconditioner).

Answer. All six variants return the same temperatures (the system is linear, so they must). They differ in cost. On the heat sink, AMG-PCG is the fastest at every measured size and for 1, 5 and 20 load cases: 9.5 s at 3.76 M DOF, 11× ahead of the next solver, with an iteration count that falls as the mesh is refined. The direct solver is the fastest only on the small, slender v1 bar and runs out of its 8 GB budget at 568 152 DOF (RCM ordering) and 1 022 979 DOF (nested dissection). PCG with Jacobi wins on the compact cube and uses the least memory. Figure 25 (section 9) is the decision chart.

The evidence is 241 runs of one official batch on an idle machine (Intel Core i5-14600K, one pinned performance core unless stated), each run in its own process, with setup and solve timed separately, every answer checked against a reference solution and every failure recorded with its cause. Every number in this report is filled in from the committed result files; the appendix lists the file, run and column behind each one.

2. From the cube to a complex object

The v1 assignment solved a 1 m × 10 mm × 10 mm bar (the notice calls it "the cube") as a 1D rod. Steady conduction with constant conductivity is linear: the finite-element equations are K·T = f with K symmetric positive definite, and every correct solver returns the same T. On simple objects the solvers therefore agree on the answer and differ only slightly in time, which is what the notice anticipated. Experiment E0 confirms it on the bar and on a cube of the same volume (Figure 1): the six variants agree on the peak temperature to within 8.5e−6 K, and the fastest solver changes with the shape. On the slender bar the direct solver wins (6.5 ms at 10 050 DOF), because a quasi-1D mesh factorises with almost no fill; on the compact cube Jacobi-PCG wins (4.5 ms) and the direct solver needs 2.5 s for the same answer. To separate the solvers properly the study needs a real 3D object with features that stress them differently: thin parts, a large conductivity contrast, convection-only boundaries, and enough unknowns to show how cost grows.

3. The object and the thermal problem

HS-7 (Figure 2) is a heat sink as used on a processor: a 60 × 40 × 4 mm aluminium base (k = 200 W/(m·K)) with seven 2 mm fins, 26 mm tall and 9 mm apart, a 20 × 20 × 0.5 mm silicon die (k = 150 W/(m·K)) dissipating P = 30 W, and between them a 0.1 mm TIM with k = 3 W/(m·K). The conductivities are chosen design inputs of typical magnitude for an aluminium alloy, silicon and a thermal paste, not measured properties of a product. Each feature is there for a solver reason: the TIM is thin and about 67 times less conductive than the base, which makes K ill-conditioned; the fins are thin plates, which shape the sparsity pattern; and the only heat path to the ambient is convection, so no temperature is fixed and the system is only weakly anchored.

Figure 2. HS-7 assembled (left) and exploded to show the TIM and the die (right). Geometry from the design definition; rendered by the study's own rasteriser.

The analysis is the same as in v1, steady conduction with heat generation, now in 3D: −∇·(k∇T) = q in each material (q = P / die volume in the die, 0 elsewhere), with convection −k ∂T/∂n = h(T − T∞) on every face exposed to air (base case h = 25 W/(m²·K), T∞ = 25 °C; experiment E3 varies h from 5 to 500) and an insulated board side (Figure 3).

Figure 3. Boundary conditions seen from above and from the board side.

4. 3D finite elements, mesh and verification

The domain is meshed with trilinear (8-node) hexahedra on a tensor grid whose lines include every face of every part, so material interfaces are exact. A single spacing s controls the mesh; it runs down a geometric ladder from 1.0 mm to 0.198 mm, roughly doubling the DOF at each step. The TIM always has one cell through its thickness, so refining the mesh changes the size of the problem but not the TIM's conditioning (Figure 4). Element matrices are exact for boxes (2×2×2 Gauss points) and are scattered straight into compressed sparse row (CSR) storage. At the finest mesh K has 96.5 M non-zeros and takes 1.22 GB; assembly takes 3.5 s.

Figure 4. Meshes at s = 1.0 and 0.5 mm, with the TIM detail.

6. How each solver was built

All three solvers take the same CSR matrix, share one set of sparse kernels (matrix–vector product, dot products, vector updates on a thread pool with fixed-size chunks, so results are bit-identical for any thread count) and report through the same interface: a setup step done once per matrix and a solve step per load case. A failure is a recorded outcome (breakdown, stagnation, over budget), never a wrong temperature. The solvers never see the mesh; a dependency check in the build enforces this, so each one works on the matrix alone (Figure 10). Figure 11 shows the three algorithms side by side.

Figure 10. Software architecture of the study (C++17, no third-party numerical code).
Figure 11. Algorithm flow: what happens in setup (once) and in solve (per load case) for each solver.

A. Sparse direct Cholesky (DIRECT-ND, DIRECT-RCM)

K is factorised as P·K·Pᵀ = L·Lᵀ and each load case costs two triangular solves [2]. The ordering P decides how much the factor fills in (Figure 12). RCM [3] narrows the band; nested dissection [4] splits the mesh graph recursively by small separators and numbers them last, which keeps the fill lower on large 3D meshes. A symbolic pass computes the exact size of L before any numeric work, so a factor that would exceed the 8 GB budget is refused in seconds instead of exhausting memory; in every run the prediction equalled the actual non-zero count. Rejected alternatives: a dense or banded solver (memory grows with n² or n·bandwidth) and a supernodal factorisation (faster, but much more code than the study needs to show the scaling).

Figure 12. Sparsity of K and of the Cholesky factor L for natural, RCM and nested-dissection orderings (HS-7, s = 2 mm; darker = denser).

B. Preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG-J, PCG-IC0)

Conjugate gradient [5] needs only matrix–vector products and a few vectors, so its memory is O(n). The number of iterations is bounded by a multiple of the square root of the condition number, and for a 3D mesh grows roughly as n1/3 [6]. Two preconditioners were built: Jacobi (divide by the diagonal; cannot fail and runs in parallel) and incomplete Cholesky IC(0) (the Cholesky factor restricted to the pattern of K), with a diagonal-shift ladder in case a pivot becomes non-positive [7], [8]. Every run also estimates the condition number κ from the CG coefficients, and stopping is not trusted to the residual alone: the error against the reference and the energy imbalance are recorded too.

C. Smoothed-aggregation algebraic multigrid (AMG, AMG-PCG)

Multigrid removes smooth error on coarser versions of the problem, which is what makes Krylov methods slow. The study's object is too thin and too uneven (0.1 mm TIM, 2 mm fins) for a geometric coarse grid, so the coarse levels are built from the matrix [9], [10]: neighbours with strong coupling are grouped into aggregates (Figure 13), each aggregate becomes one coarse unknown, the resulting interpolation is smoothed once, and the coarse matrix is the Galerkin product Pᵀ·K·P. This repeats until a few hundred unknowns remain, which are factorised by solver A. A V-cycle with symmetric Gauss–Seidel smoothing is used alone (AMG) or as the preconditioner of CG (AMG-PCG); a test checks that the cycle is symmetric and positive definite, as CG requires.

Figure 13. AMG level-0 aggregates on the HS-7 surface (each colour is one coarse unknown) and the hierarchy at s = 1 mm.

A flaw found by the experiments and fixed. The strength threshold θ₀ that decides which couplings count as "strong" had been tuned to 0.08 on a textbook 7-point finite-difference Laplacian, where every coupling is 1/6 of the diagonal. The study's trilinear hexahedra couple more weakly: on a cube-shaped cell the largest coupling is 1/16 of the diagonal, so with 0.08 every coupling was "weak", the nodes joined no aggregate and AMG degraded to plain smoothing. The official batch showed it: stand-alone AMG needed 1 992 cycles on the plain cube and stagnated on 286 k-DOF HS-7, and AMG-PCG iterations grew from 52 to 872 with refinement instead of staying flat. With θ₀ = 0.02 the cube needs 15 cycles, every AMG run converges, and AMG-PCG at 3.76 M DOF takes 23 iterations and 9.5 s instead of 237 s. All 72 AMG runs were re-run on the corrected code; the results in this report are the corrected ones, and the old rows are kept separately for comparison.

7. Comparison

Protocol. Same matrix, right-hand side, compiler flags and timing code for every solver; one thread pinned to a performance core for the main comparison (threads separately in E6); each run in a fresh process with a memory cap, a 30-minute cap and an idle check; 5, 3 or 1 repetitions depending on run time (median reported); setup and solve timed separately; iterative solvers stop at a relative residual of 10⁻¹⁰ and every answer is compared with a reference solution. All solvers are implemented to a similar level of optimisation, so the transferable results are slopes and crossovers, not absolute times.

E3: conditioning (convection, TIM conductivity and thickness)

At s = 0.5 mm (285 532 DOF) the convection coefficient was swept from 5 to 500 W/(m²·K) and the TIM conductivity from 0.5 to 200 W/(m·K), with TIM thicknesses of 0.1–0.5 mm in one or two cells. The condition-number estimate of the IC(0)-preconditioned system falls 93× from h = 5 to h = 500, yet PCG-IC0 iterations change only from 361 to 260, well below the √κ that theory allows as an upper bound. AMG-PCG stays between 23 and 40 iterations over every variant; the hardest cases have two cells across the TIM. The direct solver's cost does not depend on conditioning at all (Figures 20 and 21).

E4: the design complexity ladder

Figure 22 runs all six variants on five designs at the same spacing, from the base plate alone to HS-7 and a pin-fin variant. The direct solver's fill depends on the shape: per DOF the ND factor holds 194 entries for the plate, 197 with fins, 296 for HS-7 and 1025 for the compact cube. Thin, plate-like parts have small separators, so the direct solver is cheaper on them than on a solid block of the same size; the iterative solvers hardly notice the shape.

E5: several load cases

A factorisation (or a hierarchy) built once can be reused for every further load case, so more load cases favour solvers with expensive setup and cheap solves. For 20 load cases at 170 634 DOF the direct solver (RCM) needs 27.3 s against 30.4 s for PCG-IC0, so it overtakes PCG there; but AMG-PCG, which also reuses its setup, needs only 8.3 s. Even on the smallest mesh with 20 load cases AMG-PCG (2.99 s) stays ahead of the direct solver (3.38 s).

Figure 23. Setup and solve for 1 and 20 load cases at 171 k DOF. Data: E5_load_cases.csv.

E6: all cores

With 20 threads PCG-J speeds up 2.6× (14.6 → 5.5 s at 568 152 DOF) and gains nothing beyond 4 threads, because its kernels are limited by memory bandwidth. That narrows its gap to serial AMG-PCG (1.57 s) but does not close it. AMG-PCG itself gains little (1.38 s at 4 threads) and slows down with more threads (2.07 s at 20); its Gauss–Seidel smoother runs serially, and the cause of the slowdown was not investigated further.

E8: failures are reported, not hidden

If every face of HS-7 is insulated, no heat can leave and K is singular. All six variants detect this and report it instead of returning temperatures (Figure 24). Over the whole batch 228 runs converged, 7 direct runs were refused by the memory budget before allocating, and the 4 + 2 breakdowns are the singular cases; no run crashed, stagnated or hit a time cap.

8. Hypotheses stated before the experiments, and what was measured

The hypotheses below were written into the build specification before any official run, so the experiments could refute them. Three were confirmed or supported, three only partly, two were refuted, and one effect was not observed.

#Hypothesis (before)Outcome (after)
H1Same temperatures on both simple objects; on the slender bar direct wins clearly Partly. Same answers to 8.5e−6 K. Direct (RCM) wins the 1.8 k and 10 k DOF bars, AMG-PCG the smallest bar, PCG-J every cube (E0)
H2Direct (ND) wins below a crossover size n₁, AMG-PCG above; PCG-J never wins on time but uses least memory Refuted as stated: no crossover; AMG-PCG wins from the smallest HS-7 mesh (0.19 s vs 3.7 s for ND). PCG-J: least memory, confirmed (E2)
H3PCG iterations ∝ n1/3; AMG iterations about flat Confirmed: slopes 0.23 and 0.34 for PCG, −0.17 for AMG-PCG (E2)
H4Low Biot number, high TIM contrast and thin TIM raise κ; PCG iterations ∝ √κ; AMG rises little; direct cost unchanged Partly. κ_est changes 93× over h, PCG-IC0 iterations only 1.39×; AMG-PCG and direct nearly unchanged (E3)
H5With many load cases the direct/iterative crossover moves to larger meshes Refuted: AMG-PCG reuses its setup too and still wins for 20 load cases; direct does overtake PCG-IC0 up to 171 k DOF (E5)
H6Plate-like fins keep the direct factor's fill below that of a compact block Supported: fill per DOF 197 (fins) vs 1025 (cube) (E4)
H7On all cores PCG-J closes much of the gap to serial AMG; speed-up limited by memory bandwidth Partly: 2.6× speed-up, flat beyond 4 threads, still 3.5× slower than serial AMG-PCG (E6)
H8IC(0) breaks down without a diagonal shift on thin, stretched TIM cells Not observed: no shift needed in any run; without the shift ladder IC(0) converged in 347 iterations (E3, E8)
H9On a singular case (all faces insulated) every solver detects and reports it Confirmed: all six report a breakdown, none returns temperatures (E8)

Two refutations are the most useful results of the study. The planned story was a crossover: direct for small problems, multigrid for large ones. On a real 3D assembly with a working AMG there is no such crossover down to 56 k DOF, and the "many load cases favour direct" argument fails because multigrid amortises its setup as well. The direct solver keeps its place for small, slender or plate-like problems and as the robust fallback.

9. Which solver for which scenario

Figure 25. Decision chart: the measured fastest solver for each mesh size and number of load cases on HS-7, with its lead over the runner-up. Data: E5_load_cases.csv, E2.csv, E6.csv.
ScenarioChooseEvidence
Realistic 3D part with thin layers, any size from 10⁴ DOF up, one or many load cases AMG-PCG Fastest at every HS-7 size (E2, E5); iterations flat or falling; 11× ahead at 3.76 M DOF
Small or slender problem (quasi-1D), or any problem where a robust answer independent of conditioning matters more than time Direct Cholesky (RCM when small, ND when large) Wins the 1.8 k and 10 k DOF bars (E0); cost independent of conditioning (E3); fill small for plate-like parts (E4); memory wall at 568 k (RCM) / 1.02 M (ND) DOF in 8 GB
Compact, well-conditioned problem; least memory; many idle cores PCG-J Wins the cube (E0); lowest memory (E2); 2.6× on all cores (E6)
Iterative solver with no multigrid availablePCG-IC0 2.7× fewer iterations than PCG-J at 3.76 M DOF and faster at every size (E2); no breakdown seen anywhere (E3, E8)
Stand-alone multigridnot recommended Converges, but 2.5× slower than the same hierarchy inside CG; the TIM still costs it 365 cycles at 1 mm

Design complexity drives the choice through three properties. Size: the direct solver's time and memory grow fastest, so it is limited to small problems in 3D. Shape: thin, plate-like or slender parts factorise with less fill, which extends the direct solver's range (E0, E4). Conditioning (thin low-conductivity layers, weak convection): Krylov iterations grow with it, while the direct solver and, as a preconditioner, AMG are almost unaffected (E3). A realistic engineering assembly is large, mixed in shape and badly conditioned, which is why AMG-PCG is the default recommendation.

10. Limitations and next steps

  • All solvers are the author's own, at a similar optimisation level. Industrial direct solvers are supernodal and multithreaded and would move the direct curves down; the slopes and the memory wall would remain.
  • One machine, one object family and one physics (linear steady conduction). Process-to-process timing noise was about 10 % in the dry run; differences smaller than that are not claimed.
  • The AMG smoother is serial, so AMG-PCG does not use extra cores (E6); a parallel smoother is the obvious next step.
  • Stand-alone AMG still needs many more cycles on HS-7 than on the cube; aggregation that respects material boundaries (the TIM) would address it.
  • Not covered: transient analysis and temperature-dependent conductivity, where the matrix changes and setup reuse matters more.
