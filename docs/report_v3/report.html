Can AMG-PCG be beaten? Faster solvers for the HS-7 heat sink

Name: Aakash G S · Repository: github.com/ftaakash/UniForge, branch v3 (follow-up to the V2 report) · Draft for review · November 2026

1. Summary

Starting point. The V2 report compared direct Cholesky, preconditioned CG and smoothed-aggregation algebraic multigrid on HS-7, a seven-fin heat sink on a silicon die with a thin thermal interface layer. AMG as a CG preconditioner (AMG-PCG) was the fastest solver at every size up to 3.76 M unknowns (DOF), on one core.

Question. A literature survey after V2 named the candidates that might beat it. Can any of them, on the same machine, the same matrices and the same tolerance? And how does our AMG compare with mature libraries?

Answer. Yes, on more than one core. AMG-PCG-CH-SP, the same AMG with a parallel Chebyshev–Jacobi smoother, a single-precision hierarchy and a parallel setup, is about 1.8–2.4× faster in setup plus solve than V2's AMG-PCG on one core, and 2.0–3.1× in the solve alone, on 20 pinned threads at every HS-7 size, with the same accuracy and a third more solver memory. The one lower point, 1.75× at 1.02 M, is a noisy row whose observer-off twin gives 2.01× (sections 5 and 10). On 8 threads the total speed-up is 1.71–2.32× outside that row. For several load cases, solving them in lockstep adds another 1.28–2.53×. On one core, V2's AMG-PCG is still the right choice. Against hypre BoomerAMG and PETSc GAMG in their standard settings, our AMG-PCG is 1.27–2.30× faster on one core. The gain is in the setup; the solves are on a par.

The evidence is 314 runs of the V2 harness, all converged, plus 16 PETSc runs and a 21-run timing repeat. As in V2, each run is its own process with setup and solve timed separately, 5 timed solves (median reported), relative residual 10⁻¹⁰ and every answer checked against a reference. Every number in this report is filled in from the committed result files, and Appendix D lists the file, row and column behind each one.

2. Context: V2's answer and the V3 question

V2 found AMG-PCG fastest on HS-7 at every size, but it left two things open. Its Gauss–Seidel smoother and its setup were serial, so it gained nothing from more cores. And it was measured only against our own other solvers. The post-V2 survey (all solver families, with sources) named the candidates most likely to beat it on this problem: reduced precision in the preconditioner, polynomial (Chebyshev) smoothing, which parallelises, a parallel setup, geometric multigrid on the structured HS-7 grid, amortising one setup over several load cases, and the mature libraries hypre and PETSc as an external yardstick. V3 tests them in that order, one experiment per step. Each step is adopted only if it beats the current best on the same matrices. All runs use the V2 harness and its rules: one process per run, the same Intel Core i5-14600K, the Balanced power plan unless stated, and every row recording its source version. The full write-up of every step is in the repository's V3 experiment notes.

3. V3A: single-precision AMG hierarchy

Idea. The literature reports 1.1–1.6× from storing and applying the AMG preconditioner in single precision under a double-precision CG, because a V-cycle is limited by memory bandwidth. Result. On one pinned core the solve changed by +8.3 % at 56 015 DOF and −5.7 % at 1 022 979 DOF, the best case, with identical iterations (26) and the same error against the reference. Solver memory grew from 747 to 960 MB at 3.76 M, because float copies are kept next to the double structure. The saving is far below the literature's, which shows that the serial Gauss–Seidel sweep (a chain of dependent updates), not bandwidth, limits the V-cycle here. With 8 threads the saving was larger (−19 % at 1.02 M), but those runs were unpinned and are not re-timed.

Not adopted alone. It pointed to the real bottleneck, the serial smoother, and single precision returns in V3B as part of the adopted solver.

4. V3B: Chebyshev–Jacobi smoother

Idea. Replace the serial Gauss–Seidel smoother by a degree-2 Chebyshev polynomial in the Jacobi-scaled operator. It needs only matrix–vector products, which run on all cores, and is a standard choice in parallel AMG [1]. The upper eigenvalue bound is 1.1 times a 20-step power iteration estimate. Variants: AMG-PCG-CH (double precision) and AMG-PCG-CH-SP (single-precision hierarchy from V3A).

Result (pinned, V3H). On one core Chebyshev is slower than Gauss–Seidel, as expected. With threads, at 3 759 720 DOF the CH-SP solve takes 3.30 s on 20 threads, against 4.41 s for CH and 6.55 s for V2's AMG-PCG on one core. Single precision pays off once the smoother is parallel: CH-SP is faster than CH at every size and thread count measured, apart from one noisy row (1.87 M, 8 threads, section 10). Iterations stay at 26–27 on the large meshes. The originally published V3B thread numbers were unpinned. Five of the eight 8-thread rows were 39–45 % slow draws (section 10), and they are superseded by the table in section 5.

Adopted (as CH-SP). At this point setup became the larger share of the time at 3.76 M, which set up V3C.

5. V3C: parallel AMG setup

Idea. Profile the setup and parallelise every row-wise step: strength, the filtered matrix, the prolongator product, the Galerkin product (Gustavson's sparse product [2] with rows split over threads), the eigenvalue estimate and the float copy. Only the greedy aggregation and the transposes stay serial. Each row is computed as in the serial code, so the hierarchy is bit-identical on any thread count, which is checked by a test on 1, 4 and 8 threads. A failed shortcut. Cutting the power iteration from 20 to 10 steps under-estimated the largest eigenvalue, the preconditioner lost positive definiteness and CG broke down at 3.76 M. The default stays at 20.

6. V3D: geometric multigrid

Idea. The survey named geometric multigrid the strongest rival to AMG on structured meshes. HS-7 is a graded tensor grid with a cell mask, so a geometric hierarchy is available. Coarse nodes lie on every second grid line, fine nodes interpolate linearly along the real (graded) grid lines, and corners inside the fin gaps are dropped and the weights renormalised. Galerkin coarse operators, smoothers, single precision and the parallel setup are reused from the AMG code. Not tested: a matrix-free fine level, the survey's main argument for GMG.

Result. GMG's setup is cheaper, but its iteration count stays at 31–37 on every mesh, while AMG's falls as the mesh is refined (from 50 to 23 on one core). Full geometric coarsening of HS-7's stretched cells (the thin interface layer and the fins) is weaker than coarsening that follows the strong couplings. On one core GMG-PCG is 1.54× faster at 56 k DOF but AMG-PCG is faster from 0.57 M up (ratio 0.77 at 3.76 M). On 20 pinned threads GMG-PCG-CH-SP wins only at 94 k DOF (1.13) and 0.17 M (1.03). AMG-PCG-CH-SP is faster from 0.29 M up (ratios 0.88–0.94).

Figure 2. Ratio of AMG to GMG time (setup + solve); above 1, GMG is faster. On one core (V3D, Gauss–Seidel, pinned) GMG wins up to 0.29 M DOF. On 20 pinned threads (V3H, Chebyshev, single precision) it wins only at 94 k and 0.17 M. Data: V3D.csv, V3H.csv.

Not adopted. GMG would need semi-coarsening or line smoothing across the thin layers, or a matrix-free fine level, to compete. Both are larger steps.

7. V3E: several load cases after one setup

Idea. Engineering studies often solve one model for several loads. V2 showed that a direct solver does not pay off on HS-7 even then. V3E adds AMG-PCG-CH-SP-B, which runs L CG recurrences in lockstep. Block products and a block single-precision V-cycle read K and every hierarchy level once per iteration for all L loads, and each load keeps its own step sizes and stopping test. The same batch introduced pinned threads (section 10).

8. V3F: hypre BoomerAMG and PETSc GAMG

Question. Is our AMG close to the state of the art? hypre 3.2.1 [3] was built locally and linked as an optional solver into our harness. It runs on the same process, matrices, tolerance and repetitions: hypre PCG with one BoomerAMG V-cycle, HMIS coarsening [4], extended+i interpolation [5] (at most 4 entries per row), hybrid symmetric Gauss–Seidel, and strength threshold 0.25 (hypre's default) or 0.5 (its suggestion for 3D). PETSc 3.26.1 [6] CG with GAMG, the defaults and threshold 0.02 (our θ₀), ran on the same exported systems in a Linux VM on the same CPU. Both stop on the same relative residual.

Caveats. (1) Neither library was tuned for HS-7; the settings follow their documentation. (2) PETSc runs in a Linux VM (WSL2) while Windows ran the Balanced plan, so its times are indicative, not exactly comparable. (3) hypre uses MSVC's OpenMP 2.0, and its OpenMP threads are not pinned. (4) hypre's setup includes copying K into its own matrix format, which our solver does not need.

Result. On one core our AMG-PCG is 1.27–2.30× faster than the best external result at each size. On 6 threads AMG-PCG-CH-SP is 1.31–3.43× faster than hypre. The difference is the setup. Per solve, PETSc GAMG with threshold 0.02 matches our AMG-PCG on the large meshes (6.92 vs 6.99 s at 3.76 M), and hypre has the best solve below 0.1 M. Every external run reached the same residual, with errors against the reference at most about ten times ours (largest: hypre θ 0.5 at 1.87 M, 6.2e−10 K against 6.7e−11 K).

Reference only. Our smoothed-aggregation AMG is competitive with the state of the art on HS-7. It is ahead in setup cost, not in convergence, and a tuned hypre or PETSc could close part of that gap.

9. V3G: correctness under sanitizers, and a timing repeat

Sanitizers. Until V3G the V3 code had been built only with MSVC. The CI's two sanitizer configurations were reproduced locally under WSL with GCC 13, without pushing. The first build failed under GCC's warnings-as-errors: sign conversions in the geometric prolongator, the load-case generator and one test, and a false array-bounds warning on sorting an eight-entry array. These were fixed without any change in behaviour. Then the full suite (409 tests) passed under address and undefined-behaviour sanitizers and under the thread sanitizer with no reports. That covers the parallel setup, the lockstep block code and the bit-identity tests across thread counts. No race or undefined behaviour was found.

Timing repeat. At 3.76 M on 8 threads the same CH-SP solve had taken 4.85 s in V3B and 3.38 s in V3C, a gap larger than either run's spread. V3G rebuilt the V3B and V3C binaries from their commits. It ran them unpinned, alongside today's code unpinned and pinned, alternating round by round, on both power plans.

V3B's own binary reached 3.36 s in one round, and today's code unpinned took 5.59 s in another. So the gap was neither the code (start vector of the eigenvalue estimate, setup change) nor the power plan; it was where Windows placed the unpinned threads. A likely mechanism is helpers landing on efficiency cores, but this was not measured per core. Pinning narrows the spread to about ±2 %.

10. V3H: thread runs re-timed with pinned threads

Because V3B, V3C and V3D timed their thread runs unpinned, all of them were repeated in one batch with today's code and P-core-first pinning: performance cores first, one thread per core before the SMT siblings, efficiency cores last. V2's AMG-PCG on one core ran in the same batch, so every ratio comes from one batch. The plan was Balanced, as in the originals, so only the pinning changed. That is 56 runs, all converged. The one-core baseline reproduced V3C to within 5 %.

  • No verdict changes. CH-SP stays the fastest variant at every size and thread count, and GMG still loses from 0.29 M up. The 3.76 M exception in V3C ("8 threads beat 20") was noise.
  • Most of V3C's points were slightly fast draws (pinned is a few per cent slower below 1.02 M). V3B's 8-thread rows were the slow ones, for example 6.29 s then and 4.42 s now for CH at 3.76 M.
  • Pinning narrows the spread but does not remove it. From 0.57 M up, 4 of 28 pinned runs still had one or two slow solves out of five. The worst was CH-SP on 8 threads at 1.87 M, 1.78–4.11 s within one run. Below 0.57 M, solves take 0.07–0.25 s and most runs had a slow outlier. They are marked in the tables and, where possible, cross-checked against the observer-off twin.
  • Consequence for every claim in this report: a gap under about 10 % from a single run is not significant. Only gaps larger than that, or ones confirmed by a twin or a repeat, are claimed.

Solver memory is the preconditioner, hierarchy and work vectors, without the system matrix and mesh. Peak process memory is dominated by those and barely moves for one load (3.0 GB for AMG-PCG, 3.0 GB for CH-SP). The adopted solver costs a third more solver memory. Lockstep memory grows with every load case (each adds about 6 double and 5 float vectors), so L = 20 needs 6.1× the memory of solving them one by one; the 8 GB budget would refuse L = 32 at this size.

13. Limitations and next steps

  • One machine, one object family, one physics (linear steady conduction on HS-7). The speed-ups depend on this CPU's core layout and memory bandwidth (RAM ran at 4800 MT/s, below its rating, kept constant for comparability).
  • Timing noise. Even pinned, one run in seven from 0.57 M up, and most smaller runs, had outlier solves; single-run gaps under 10 % are not claimed (section 10).
  • External references are untuned, and PETSc ran in a VM. A tuned hypre (aggressive coarsening, smaller stencils) or GAMG setup reuse could close part of our setup advantage.
  • GPU: not done. The survey's GPU candidates (AMG on a GPU, mixed precision there) were not attempted in this round; every result here is CPU-only.
  • CFD: not started. Flow problems (non-symmetric, saddle-point systems) need different solvers (e.g. GMRES with block or AMG preconditioners). Nothing in V3 measures them, and none of these results should be read as a claim about CFD.
  • Other open steps: a matrix-free fine level for GMG, semi-coarsening across thin layers, and E-core-aware placement of threads beyond the six performance cores.

Appendix B. Effort and use of AI tools

As in V2, the software, experiments, figures and this report were developed with an AI coding assistant (Claude, Anthropic), which wrote and committed the work phase by phase. The author set the direction, approved each step (V3A to V3H and this report), and made the decisions the assistant proposed with their trade-offs. These included keeping the RAM at 4800 MT/s, using the High performance plan only during batches, pinning threads, downloading hypre, installing PETSc in WSL, and re-timing the unpinned runs. Mistakes that were caught are recorded with how they were found, among them: a wrong eigenvalue shortcut (section 5), an unexplained timing gap that was first attributed to the code (section 9), a library-runtime mismatch when linking hypre, the compile errors MSVC had not flagged (section 9), and several miscounted or misstated ranges in draft write-ups, corrected from the CSVs before commit. A dated log of prompts, outputs, verification and errors is kept in the repository (AI-use log).

Time. Measured from the timestamps of the assistant sessions, counting gaps of at most 10 minutes between consecutive messages as active time (unattended batch runs are not counted). The working session that built V3A–V3H and this report was active for 1.6 h on 27 November (survey and V3A–V3F, 19:21–23:18) and 1.2 h on 28 November (V3F write-up to this draft, 00:21–01:53), 2.8 h in all. A second session, used for planning and review, was active for 0.65 h, partly at the same time. The 27–28 November batches ran unattended and are not counted, nor is the author's own time reading results and this report.
